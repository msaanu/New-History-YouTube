# Life as a Geisha in Old Japan — story-first rewrite

## Nature and historical limits
A reconstructed adult working life under the earlier Yoshiwara registry regime described retrospectively by Joseph De Becker. The principal scene is placed broadly in the late eighteenth century, following the reported founding of the registry in 1779; this is not a documented person's exact day. The account does not establish the precise duration or uniform enforcement of every rule. Not all geisha throughout Japan shared these arrangements.

The invitation, practice mistake, conversation, assistance between colleagues and private responses are dramatized connective scenes. The protagonist does not actually receive the suspension: its effects remain conditional. Names of documented performers occur in historical background, not as invented acquaintances. New Year rounds are a separate recurring occasion, not silently inserted into the same evening. The epilogue explicitly moves to other women and later decades.

The narration leaves bibliographic and methodological explanations here. It must be accompanied by the description disclosure below. Removing spoken attribution does not upgrade the reliability of the evidence.

No Masuda memoir material was read or used in this rewrite. No twentieth-century hot-spring experiences are silently assigned to an eighteenth-century woman. No new full scholarly text was obtained. This version uses previously accessible historical texts and the scholarly abstract already read.

## Description-ready evidence notice
This episode follows a composite adult geisha through a reconstructed working life in Edo's Yoshiwara. Historical details about the earlier registry regime are drawn from a later account, Joseph De Becker's history of the quarter; original regulations were not independently inspected, and the rules are not presented as universal across Japan. Private reactions, dialogue-free interactions and connective scenes are dramatized. The possible suspension is conditional, not a recorded punishment involving a named woman. The closing section moves explicitly into Meiji and other women's experiences. Geisha and courtesans were distinct occupations. We do not endorse the prejudices in historical sources.

List S1–S5 with links. Label Stanley “public abstract consulted.” Do not claim full-text academic verification. Further-reading books not actually read must remain labelled further reading.
