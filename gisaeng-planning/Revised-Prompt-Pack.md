# Asia’s Past — revised reusable prompt pack
Updated 21 October 2026. This revision incorporates the latest approved round-face style. It supersedes the original pack wherever style or editing instructions conflict.

## Current production decisions — apply throughout all prompts
- No images, voiceover or video are requested at the present research stage.
- Round-faced characters are the default; the detailed anime-style thumbnails are an optional future experiment only.
- Rich Korean historical surroundings, fully coloured period garments; no generic mixed-Asian designs.
- Narration only; no music/effects. Hard cuts; no zooms, pans or transitions. 1920×1080, 16:9, full-scene framing.
- Every meaningful narrated clause needs a visual assignment. Specific nouns and actions should receive object/action depictions where useful. No arbitrary 1–2-second cut rule, no generic image cycling.
- Brief contextual statements on plain ivory; canonical spellings rather than phonetic TTS input. Names appear when introduced, not prematurely.
- Preserve generation manifests and approved masters. Small batches, one separate scene image per asset; never enlarge contact-sheet panels for the final edit.
- Do not claim that a prompt overrides tool limits or creates unlimited bulk generation. Actual image-generation capability must be available in the chat used.
- Preview first; obtain project-specific approval before MP4 export. Preserve the original-quality export separately from compressed download copies.
- Verify whether the current session is local or cloud. A file in /workspace is not a file saved to the user’s Windows laptop. This preparation session remains in the cloud.
- User may supply approved recordings; do not regenerate them by default or require voice cloning.

NON-NEGOTIABLE HISTORICAL STANDARD
Every substantive historical claim must be traceable to evidence that has actually been inspected.
- Never invent sources, URLs, quotations, page numbers, translations, archival identifiers, or scholarly consensus.
- Search snippets, AI answers, rival videos, and historical dramas are not sufficient evidence.
- A webpage existing does not mean it supports a claim.
- Distinguish a documented event, a source’s allegation, a historian’s interpretation, a disputed account, and an unknown.
- Qualify interpretation and disagreement explicitly. Do not turn “a chronicler alleged” into “this happened.”
- Separate laws or prescribed rules from evidence of actual practice.
- Check dates, calendar conventions, ages, currencies, ranks, and names.

The Mihrimah script: a story driven by a day
This script doesn’t depend on a single immediate crisis. It uses a reconstructed day, with each activity revealing a different aspect of the woman’s position.
Its crucial disclosure comes early:
“Nobody wrote down a single day of your life, but you left letters, a mosque, and a foundation deed.”
That tells viewers the day is being reconstructed. The script then moves through getting dressed, correspondence, financial administration, a visit, travel, and evening work.
Three devices connect those activities:
A recurring theme: “Every hour has a price.”
A numerical question: how many villages support the foundation?
An underlying concern: family and political vulnerability beneath material privilege.
The numerical question receives its answer around 4:22, rather than being withheld until the ending. The script keeps another, larger question alive: what does this apparently privileged life actually demand?
Around 9:45, it shifts from the reconstructed day to later events and legacy. That gives the episode a second scale: first a day, then a lifetime.
These are two different structures—not one formula to repeat:
Crisis: something happens → your position becomes uncertain → events unfold → consequences differ.
Daily life: you undertake a task → its rules become visible → its cost emerges → the next task deepens the picture.
What we should adapt—and where we must be more careful
We should adopt the second-person perspective, concrete actions, recurring objects, and explanations that arrive when the viewer needs them.
But the references sometimes move from documentary material into imagined detail: an exact sensation, an internal worry, a private exchange, or activities placed together on one particular day. A general reconstruction disclaimer does not verify every detail that follows.
For Asia’s Past, we can write immersive history without pretending to possess that knowledge:
Use documented actions as actions.
Explain documented restrictions through their consequences for the person.
Attribute reports and allegations naturally.
Label a reconstructed sequence as reconstructed.
Leave out invented dialogue and unsupported internal thoughts.
Don’t assign events from different dates to one day without making that construction clear.
The research should support the storytelling from underneath. It should not make the narration sound like a research checklist.
